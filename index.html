Case study · Retention · Klaviyo

Winning back customers without burning the margin

ZeeWear · Klaviyo + Shopify · Email + SMS

A six-touchpoint win-back program for a lapsed apparel audience. The discount only appears once two cheaper attempts have failed, a 10% holdout proves whether any of it worked, and anyone still silent at the end is suppressed rather than mailed forever.

BrandZeeWear
PlatformKlaviyo + Shopify
ChannelsEmail + SMS
Touchpoints6 over 18 days
Control10% holdout

On scope, plainly. ZeeWear is a case-study brand I built to document lifecycle architecture end to end. The segment logic, holdout design, branching and sunset policy are real and reproducible in any Klaviyo account. There is no live revenue to report, because there was no live revenue.

The brief — four things this flow has to do

01Win back lapsed buyers

Re-engage customers silent for 90 days — no purchase, no open, no site visit.

02Protect the margin

Recover revenue through escalating incentives rather than opening with a discount.

03Prove the lift is real

Hold back 10% so recovered revenue is measured against a clean baseline.

04Protect deliverability

Suppress unresponsive profiles so dead weight never drags sender reputation down.

Segment entry rule

// Segment: 90 Days Lapsed Customer
What someone has done → Placed Order → at least 1 over all time
AND What someone has done → Placed Order → 0 times in the last 90 days
AND What someone has done → Opened Email → 0 times in the last 90 days
AND What someone is in → list: ZeeWear Apparel
AND If someone can → receive email marketing

Why 10% get nothing (holdout)

A win-back flow will always report revenue because some lapsed customers were going to come back on their own. A random 10% receive nothing at all. The gap between the two groups is the only genuinely incremental number.

The sequence — six touchpoints

  • Day 0 · Email 1 — “We miss you” (0% discount, warm tone, brand reminder)
  • Day 3 · Email 2 — Personalised product match (0% discount, Shopify catalogue feed, last-purchased category)
  • Day 7 · Email 3 — Price sensitivity test (A/B split: 15% vs 20%, live countdown, code expires 48h)
  • Day 7 · SMS — Channel switch (trackable personalised link, pre-loaded checkout, STOP to end)
  • Day 12 · Email 4 — Final notice (15% expiring, minimal layout)
  • Day 13–14 · Email 5 — Break-up email (“Is it time to say goodbye?”, conditional split 36h, suppression after 5 days)

Sunset policy

Profiles that stay unresponsive after the break-up email get an unengaged tag, pulled out of active segments and into suppression. Sending only to engaged subscribers lifts open rates and keeps the domain out of spam.

What I’d check on a live account

  • Is 90 days the right lapse window? It should come from actual repeat-purchase intervals.
  • Does 20% actually beat 15%? The A/B answers it, but until it runs the ladder is a hypothesis.
  • Is SMS on the same day as Email 3 too aggressive for a cold profile?
  • Frequency collision — a profile in this flow could also receive scheduled campaigns.

Newsletter · 64K List · Beehiiv

Rebuilding a 64K list around behaviour, not batch

Content Marketing & Email Operations Coordinator · Contract, Remote (Austin, TX)

The bottleneck

A 64,000+ contact list was being treated as a single audience. Every subscriber received the same content on the same schedule regardless of how recently they had opened, clicked, or bought — so engaged readers and long-dormant addresses were sent to identically. Open rate had settled at 33.34% and stopped moving.

Tactical solution

  • Segmented by engagement recency — separating recently active subscribers from those who hadn’t opened in months, so send volume matched real interest instead of list size.
  • Split by lifecycle stage rather than signup date, so new subscribers, regulars, and lapsed contacts received appropriately different content.
  • Ran a three-element A/B test every cycle — subject lines, thumbnail and header images, and CTA placement — with variants defined before anything was scheduled.
  • Fed each result into the next send, so the winning variant became the following week’s control rather than a one-off observation.

Campaign Operations · Weekly Cadence

A send calendar that doesn’t slip

Content Marketing & Email Operations Coordinator · Contract, Remote (Austin, TX)

The bottleneck

Shipping four campaigns a week — including a standing Tuesday product newsletter — leaves no room for improvised QA. A broken link, a bad merge tag, or an unsuppressed segment reaches tens of thousands of people at once, and there is no second pass once it’s out.

Tactical solution

  • Segment first — the audience is pulled by behaviour and lifecycle stage before any copy is written, so the message is built for a defined group.
  • Build with variants pre-defined — copy, subject line, and template assembled with the test set decided before scheduling, never bolted on afterwards.
  • Fixed pre-send checklist — links, merge tags, rendering, and suppression rules verified the same way every time. That discipline came directly from raising compliance cases at HSBC and reconciling invoices on SAP at TCS and Genpact.
  • Report and recycle — opens, clicks, and deliverability reviewed after each send and fed straight into the next brief.

Lifecycle Automation · Klaviyo

Automations that run without supervision

Content Marketing & Email Operations Coordinator · Contract, Remote (Austin, TX)

The bottleneck

Campaigns reach whoever is on the list this week. Flows reach people at the moment their behaviour says they’re ready — but only if the trigger and timing are built around that behaviour. Blanket triggers send the right message at the wrong moment, which trains subscribers to ignore the sender.

Tactical solution

  • Welcome sequence built as a staged introduction rather than a single confirmation, so new subscribers arrive with context before the first promotional send.
  • Abandoned-cart flow triggered on cart activity with delay timing tuned to purchase intent, not a fixed interval applied to everyone.
  • Retention sequence targeting subscribers whose engagement had dropped, segmented by how long they had been inactive rather than treating all lapsed contacts the same.
  • Segmentation and suppression rules applied across all three, so a subscriber in one flow isn’t simultaneously receiving a conflicting campaign.
